Trust Recalibration Simulator

An AI assistant silently becomes less reliable at trial 50. Each simulated user updates their trust after every outcome and stops relying on the AI once their trust has fallen far enough. How quickly do they notice, and who never does?

How the model works

Every user starts with the same trust T0. After each trial the AI is either right (O = 1) or wrong (O = 0), with probability set by its current reliability, and trust moves a fraction α of the way towards the outcome:

Tt+1 = Tt + α (Ot − Tt)

A user stops relying on the AI the first time their trust has fallen more than θ below where it started. Each user gets their own α and θ, drawn from normal distributions with the means and spreads you set (clipped to α ∈ [0.01, 0.99], θ ∈ [0.05, 0.99]).

The two conditions differ only in θ. The model's hypothesis is that presenting an AI as warm or human-like doesn't stop people registering its errors (α is unchanged), but raises how much trust they need to lose before they act on it (θ is higher). The model doesn't simulate an anthropomorphic interface. The higher threshold stands in for one proposed mechanism.

Outcome measures. Switching is possible from the first trial, so some users give up on an AI that is still reliable. These are false alarms. Everyone else is at risk when degradation starts. For them, switching delay is how many trials after trial 50 they switch, and exposure counts post-drift trials of continued reliance. Exposure stops at trial 100, so for users who never switch it is a lower bound.

Extension: feedback visibility. This isn't in the original model. Each outcome is seen with probability v. If it isn't seen, trust doesn't change, but the user is still relying on the AI, so those trials still count as exposure. At v = 1 this is the original model. It's a simple stand-in for settings where users can't check most of the AI's answers.

What this is and isn't. It is a conceptual model, checked against qualitative patterns from the trust and automation-bias literature. It is not fitted to any dataset or validated against individual human behaviour, and exposure counts trials of reliance, not harm.

Relation to the notebook. This page reimplements the notebook's model in JavaScript with a different random number generator, so individual runs won't match the notebook's numbers digit for digit. A script in the repo (validation/compare.py) checks that the two implementations agree on every outcome measure across 5,000 simulated users per condition.
